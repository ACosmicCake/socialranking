Methodology

How every number on the results page is calculated, which data it comes from, and where it falls short.

Principles · Income · Education · Living standards · Financial security · Wealth · Location · SES index · Self-placement · Coverage · What changed · Reproducing the data

Principles

  • Objective questions only, from real surveys. Each question is the item (or a close adaptation) that produced the statistic we compare you with. The questionnaire page lists the source for every item.
  • One comparison per dimension. Socioeconomic status is usually measured through income, education and occupation {{ cite("apa_ses") }}. We add living standards, financial security and wealth, which international surveys measure well, and leave out occupation, which has no comparable global data.
  • Percentile = share of people worse off than you. For categorical answers we use the mid-rank: everyone in lower categories plus half of your own category.
  • No invented numbers. Country-level data are generated by a script from pinned public datasets, and global headline figures are taken from the latest official reports, with links.

1. Income

What is compared

The World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform (PIP) ranks people by household income or consumption per person per day, in international dollars at 2021 purchasing-power parity (PPP). We use PIP's September 2026 release ({{ inc.pip_version }}) {{ cite("pip") }} and convert your answer into the same unit:

daily income per person = household income × (12 if monthly) / 365 / household size
2021 PPP $ per day      = daily income per person / (prices this year ÷ prices in 2021) / PPP factor for 2021
  • Prices this year vs 2021 compounds the country's annual average consumer-price inflation from 2022 to {{ g.prices.last_year }} in the {{ g.prices.source }} {{ cite("imf_weo") }}. The figure for {{ g.prices.last_year }} is the IMF's estimate. Where the IMF has no figure for the latest one or two years, we carry the last rate forward and say so on your results.
  • PPP factor is local currency per international dollar in 2021: PIP's own factor where the country has a survey, which keeps the conversion consistent with the distribution, and otherwise the World Bank's private-consumption PPP (WDI PA.NUS.PRVT.PP) {{ cite("wdi") }}. For currencies replaced at a fixed rate since the survey (Croatia and Bulgaria to the euro, Sierra Leone's new leone, Belarus and Mauritania), the factor is converted to today's currency. The build checks PIP against WDI for every country and flags any remaining mismatch.
  • Venezuela and Zimbabwe (currency changes without a fixed rate), countries where inflation is projected above {{ (g.prices.max_current_inflation * 100) | round | int }}% this year, and economies with no price data (e.g. Cuba, North Korea, Syria, several small territories) can only use the range question.

The national distributions

For every economy, PIP reports the income below which 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20% ... 90%, 95%, 98% and 99% of people live, lined up to {{ inc.reference_year }}. Countries surveyed in another year are projected with national-accounts growth, and the few without any household survey are modelled by the World Bank (shown as "model estimate" on your results). We interpolate between these 15 points in log income and extend above the 99th percentile with a Pareto tail fitted to the 95th-99th percentile segment.

The global distribution

PIP publishes the share of the world's population below any line. To get one smooth curve, including the top tail, we:

  1. mix the {{ inc.countries_in_mixture }} national distributions, weighted by their {{ inc.reference_year }} population (together {{ (100 * inc.population_in_mixture / inc.world_population) | pct }} of the world);
  2. calibrate the mixture so it passes exactly through PIP's official world headcounts at {{ inc.anchors | length }} lines from $1 to $150 a day, keeping the mixture's shape between and beyond those points.

Limitations

  • Household surveys under-report the very top of the distribution, so percentiles above about 99 are uncertain.
  • Many countries (including India, China and Vietnam) measure consumption, not income. Consumption is lower than income for better-off households, so if you report income in those countries you'll rank somewhat higher than an equivalent survey respondent.
  • The distributions describe {{ inc.reference_year }}. Your income is measured in today's prices and deflated to 2021, so any real growth since {{ inc.reference_year }} makes your result slightly flattering.
  • About {{ poverty.people | people }} people lived below the $3.00 extreme-poverty line in {{ poverty.year }} {{ cite(poverty.cite) }}, matching the {{ (inc.anchors[2].share_below * 100) | pct }} share in our data.

2. Education

Your highest completed level is compared with adults aged {{ g.education.ages }} in the Barro-Lee dataset ({{ g.education.year }} estimates for {{ g.education.countries_covered }} countries) {{ cite("barro_lee") }}. Barro-Lee's cumulative categories are split into seven exclusive levels (e.g. "some secondary" = attained secondary minus completed secondary). The world shares are population-weighted:

3. Living standards

Seven household services, each compared with the share of the world's population without it:

{% for key, a in access.items() %}{% endfor %}
ServiceShare withoutPeople withoutYearSource
{{ a.label }}{{ (a.share_without * 100) | pct(0) }}{{ a.people_without | people }}{{ a.year }}{{ cite(a.cite) }}

If a share s of people lack a service, having it puts you at the mid-rank (s + 1)/2, and lacking it puts you at s/2. Your living-standards score is the average of these over the services you answered. This needs no assumption about how deprivations overlap. We also report a strict lower bound: the largest s among the services you have, since at least that many people lack something you have.

Limitations: these are global figures with no country breakdown in this app. "Safely managed" water also requires water free from contamination, and sanitation requires safe treatment of waste, neither of which a respondent can verify. The health item simplifies SDG 3.8.2.

4. Financial security

Gallup's Basic Needs Vulnerability study {{ cite("gallup_2020") }} asked adults in 142 countries whether they had been unable to afford food or shelter in the past 12 months, and whether they had friends or family to rely on. Gallup reports the high-vulnerability share ("about one in seven", 750 million adults); the 39% and 47% shares come from press coverage of the same index {{ cite("upi_gallup_2020") }}:

{% for k in gallup_order %}{% set v = gallup[k] %}{% endfor %}
GroupDefinitionShare of adults
{{ v.label }}{{ v.definition }}{{ (v.share * 100) | pct(0) }}

Your security score is the mid-rank of your group. We also report:

  • the Food Insecurity Experience Scale (8 yes/no items) {{ cite("fies") }}. FAO classifies severity with a Rasch model. We use the common raw-score approximation (0 secure, 1-3 mild, 4-6 moderate, 7-8 severe) {{ cite("fies_raw_score") }} and compare it with {{ (food.moderate_or_severe_share * 100) | pct }} ({{ food.moderate_or_severe_people | people }}) moderately or severely food insecure in {{ food.year }} {{ cite(food.cite) }};
  • account ownership ({{ (findex.account_share * 100) | pct(0) }} of adults worldwide) and saving from the Global Findex {{ cite(findex.cite) }}. Findex doesn't headline a worldwide share of adults who saved any money in 2024, so for saving we only show context: {{ (findex.saved_in_account_share_lmic * 100) | pct(0) }} of adults in low- and middle-income economies saved in a financial account.

Limitations: the Gallup data date from 2019, before the pandemic. The FIES raw-score bands only approximate FAO's probabilistic classification.

UBS's release says the two lowest bands each hold "just over 41%" of adults and 1.5% hold more than $1 million, so we use 41%, 41% and 1.5%, with the $100,000-$1 million band as the remainder. Only four bands are published, so the result is a wide range. We report its midpoint.

6. Location (context only)

We rank economies by GDP per person (current US$, latest year) and report the share of the world's population living in poorer economies {{ cite("wdi") }}. The original version of this quiz used the country as a separate score. It is now context only, because your income is already adjusted to your country's prices and compared globally, so a separate country score would double-count it.

7. The SES index

The SES index is the unweighted mean of the dimension percentiles you answered: income, education, living standards, financial security and, optionally, wealth. Averaging percentiles does not give a percentile, because no dataset measures all of these for the same people. Read the index as a summary of your position across dimensions. The income percentile is the best-measured single number.

8. Self-placement

The MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status {{ cite("macarthur") }} asks people to place themselves on a 10-rung ladder. It isn't scored. We show it next to your measured rung (your SES index divided into tenths) because people's sense of their standing is usually anchored on the people around them.

Data coverage

  • {{ coverage.economies }} economies in the country list (the World Bank's 217 economies plus Taiwan, which PIP covers).
  • {{ coverage.income }} with a national income/consumption distribution; {{ coverage.exact }} accept an exact local-currency income ({{ coverage.prices }} have price data).
  • {{ coverage.education }} with national education data; {{ coverage.gdp }} with GDP per person.
  • World population {{ g.world_population | people }} ({{ g.world_population_year }}).

What changed from the original quiz

  • The old result averaged only the education and daily-income answers. Access and country were calculated but never used. All dimensions now count, and each is shown.
  • Missing commas in the old country lists silently merged "Lithuania" with "Switzerland" and "Thailand" with "Cuba". Trailing spaces and tabs ("India ", "Costa Rica ", "Jordan") and mismatched names (Burkina, Syria, El Salvador, Congo DR) left those countries unscored, and South Korea was listed as low income. Country data now come from ISO codes.
  • Daily income was asked in unadjusted dollars. It's now converted with each country's inflation and PPP factor and placed on the World Bank's global distribution, in the 2021 prices the World Bank adopted in June 2025.
  • The quiz no longer depends on a server-side session counter, which broke when users pressed Back or used two tabs.
  • Statistics were updated to the latest reports (2024-2026), and the questions use the surveys' own wording.

Reproducing the data

All country-level data are built by scripts/build_data.py directly from the World Bank (PIP and WDI) and IMF APIs, with the PIP release pinned, plus pinned commits of the Barro-Lee and ISO code files (see sources). The data were last built on {{ g.built }}. The cited global figures live in ses/facts.py. scripts/build_site.py renders this static site, and the scoring runs in your browser (static/js/ses.js), so your answers never leave your device.
