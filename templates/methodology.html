Methodology

How every number on the results page is calculated, which data it comes from, and where it falls short.

Principles · Income · Around the world · Over time · Education · Work · Living standards · Multidimensional poverty · Financial security · Wealth · Your country · SES index · Self-placement · Coverage · Languages · What changed · Reproducing the data

Principles

  • Objective questions only, from real surveys. Each question is the item (or a close adaptation) that produced the statistic we compare you with. The questionnaire page lists the source for every item.
  • The classic pillars, and more. Socioeconomic status is usually measured through income, education and occupation {{ cite("apa_ses") }}. We cover all three (occupation through the ILO's status in employment) and add living standards, multidimensional poverty, financial security and wealth.
  • Compare at every level we have data for. The world, your region, your income group, your country, urban or rural areas, people of your age and sex, and earlier years.
  • Percentile = share of people worse off than you. For categorical answers we use the mid-rank: everyone in lower categories plus half of your own category.
  • No invented numbers. Country-level data are generated by a script straight from the World Bank, IMF and other official sources, and global headline figures are taken from the latest official reports, with links.

1. Income

What is compared

The World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform (PIP) ranks people by household income or consumption per person per day, in international dollars at 2021 purchasing-power parity (PPP). We use PIP's September 2026 release ({{ inc.pip_version }}) {{ cite("pip") }} and convert your answer into the same unit:

daily income per person = household income × (12 if monthly) / 365 / household size
2021 PPP $ per day      = daily income per person / (prices this year ÷ prices in 2021) / PPP factor for 2021
  • Prices this year vs 2021 compounds the country's annual average consumer-price inflation from 2022 to {{ g.prices.last_year }} in the {{ g.prices.source }} {{ cite("imf_weo") }}. The figure for {{ g.prices.last_year }} is the IMF's estimate. Where the IMF has no figure for the latest one or two years, we carry the last rate forward and say so on your results.
  • PPP factor is local currency per international dollar in 2021: PIP's own factor where the country has a survey, which keeps the conversion consistent with the distribution, and otherwise the World Bank's private-consumption PPP (WDI PA.NUS.PRVT.PP) {{ cite("wdi") }}. For currencies replaced at a fixed rate since the survey (Croatia and Bulgaria to the euro, Sierra Leone's new leone, Belarus and Mauritania), the factor is converted to today's currency. The build checks PIP against WDI for every country and flags any remaining mismatch.
  • Venezuela and Zimbabwe (currency changes without a fixed rate), countries where inflation is projected above {{ (g.prices.max_current_inflation * 100) | round | int }}% this year, and economies with no price data (e.g. Cuba, North Korea, Syria, several small territories) can only use the range question.

The national distributions

For every economy, PIP reports the income below which 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20% ... 90%, 95%, 98% and 99% of people live, lined up to {{ inc.reference_year }}. Countries surveyed in another year are projected with national-accounts growth, and the few without any household survey are modelled by the World Bank (shown as "model estimate" on your results). We interpolate between these 15 points in log income and extend above the 99th percentile with a Pareto tail fitted to the 95th-99th percentile segment.

The global distribution

PIP publishes the share of the world's population below any line. To get one smooth curve, including the top tail, we:

  1. mix the {{ inc.countries_in_mixture }} national distributions, weighted by their {{ inc.reference_year }} population (together {{ (100 * inc.population_in_mixture / inc.world_population) | pct }} of the world);
  2. calibrate the mixture so it passes exactly through PIP's official world headcounts at {{ inc.anchors | length }} lines from $1 to $150 a day, keeping the mixture's shape between and beyond those points.

Before calibration, the mixture is already within a few tenths of a percentage point of the official figures:

Income or spending

PIP measures income in some countries and consumption (spending, including food a household grows) in others; your results say which. You can enter either. If yours doesn't match your country's survey, the results say so: better-off households spend less than they earn, so entering income in a consumption country overstates your national rank, and vice versa. No adjustment is applied, because there is no reliable cross-country factor.

How precise is your rank?

Survey research finds that people misreport household income substantially, and often don't know exactly what other household members earn. For an exact answer we therefore also show the world and national ranks you would have at 20% less and 20% more income. Where the distribution is dense (around the world median) the range is wide; near the top it is narrow.

Can you afford a healthy diet?

The World Bank's Food Prices for Nutrition data {{ cite("fpn") }} give, for {{ coverage.healthy_diet }} economies, the cost of the cheapest diet that meets national dietary guidelines, from retail prices, and the share of people who can't afford it (world, {{ g.healthy_diet.world.year }}: {{ (g.healthy_diet.world.unaffordable_share * 100) | pct }}, {{ g.healthy_diet.world.unaffordable_people | people }} people). Following the FAO and World Bank method used in SOFI {{ cite("sofi_2026") }}, a diet is unaffordable when it costs more than {{ (g.healthy_diet.income_share * 100) | pct(0) }} of income, the average share of income that people in low-income countries spend on food. We convert the local-currency cost with the same price index and PPPs as your income, so the two are directly comparable. Where that disagrees with FPN's own PPP cost by more than 25% (a currency redenomination or very high inflation: {{ g.healthy_diet.outliers | map(attribute=0) | join(", ") }}), we use FPN's PPP cost deflated by US inflation instead.

What it takes to move up

We invert the distributions to give the income at the median, the top 10% and the top 1% of your country and of the world, in 2021 international dollars per person per day. For an exact answer they are converted back into your currency at today's prices for your household's size and period, so you can read them as "my household would need this much a month".

Your country over time

For each economy we also keep PIP's lined-up median and share below $3.00 a day for every year since 1990, and show them next to your income, along with the first year the national median reached your income.

Urban and rural distributions

Where PIP publishes separate urban and rural distributions (China in the current release), we also rank you within your area. Other countries are national only.

Limitations

  • Household surveys under-report the very top of the distribution, so percentiles above about 99 are uncertain.
  • Many countries (including India, China and Vietnam) measure consumption, not income. Consumption is lower than income for better-off households, so if you report income in those countries you'll rank somewhat higher than an equivalent survey respondent.
  • The distributions describe {{ inc.reference_year }}. Your income is measured in today's prices and deflated to 2021, so any real growth since {{ inc.reference_year }} makes your result slightly flattering.
  • About {{ poverty.people | people }} people lived below the $3.00 extreme-poverty line in {{ poverty.year }} {{ cite(poverty.cite) }}, matching the {{ (inc.anchors[2].share_below * 100) | pct }} share in our data.

4. Education

Your highest completed level is one of the nine ISCED 2011 levels that UNESCO reports for adults aged 25 and over {{ cite("uis_attainment") }}. The World Bank publishes them as cumulative shares ("at least primary", "at least lower secondary" ... "at least doctoral"). We take each country's latest year since 2010 that reports primary, lower secondary, upper secondary and bachelor's, and fill missing finer levels: post-secondary = short-cycle (none extra), short-cycle = bachelor's, and master's and doctoral from pooled ratios across countries (master's/bachelor's {{ g.education.fill_ratios.master_over_bachelor }}, doctoral/master's {{ g.education.fill_ratios.doctoral_over_master }}). {{ g.education.countries_wdi }} economies have this data; {{ g.education.countries_barro_lee }} more use Barro-Lee 2015 {{ cite("barro_lee") }}, mapped onto the nine levels. Together they cover {{ (100 * g.education.population_25_plus_covered / g.education.population_25_plus_world) | pct }} of the world's adults aged 25+, and the world shares are weighted by each country's population aged 25+:

{% for l in g.education.levels %}{% endfor %}
LevelShare of adults 25+

Women and men. If you give your sex, you are also ranked among women or men aged 25+, using the same attainment indicators reported separately by sex, for the world (weighted by population 25+) and your country.

People of your age and sex. If you give your age (25-64), you are also compared with your 10-year age group, and with your sex if you give it, using Barro-Lee's {{ g.education.groups_year }} estimates. These have only four broad levels (no schooling or incomplete primary; primary; secondary; tertiary). We map post-secondary non-tertiary to secondary, which is an approximation.

5. Work

Your main activity follows the ILO's International Classification of Status in Employment {{ cite("ilo_wdi") }}. The ILO's modelled estimates give the share of the world's (and each country's) workers who are employees, employers, own-account workers or contributing family workers. Own-account work is derived as vulnerable employment minus contributing family work. We also show sector shares (agriculture, industry, services), unemployment and labour force participation. If you give your sex, the same shares are shown for women or for men, worldwide and in your country.

The score uses the ILO's job-quality split: own-account and contributing family work count as vulnerable employment ({{ g.aggregates.WLD.wdi["SL.EMP.VULN.ZS"][1] | pct }} of the world's workers in {{ g.aggregates.WLD.wdi["SL.EMP.VULN.ZS"][0] }}). Your mid-rank among workers is the status rank. On its own this is a coarse two-level measure, and it's scored only for people who are working.

Occupation. If you're working, you can pick your ISCO-08 major group {{ cite("isco_08") }}. ISCO-08 assigns each group a skill level, from 1 (simple, routine tasks) to 4 (complex problem-solving, usually needing a degree): managers 3-4 (we use 4), professionals 4, technicians and associate professionals 3, clerical, service and sales, skilled agricultural, craft and machine-operator groups 2, and elementary occupations 1. The armed forces span several levels and aren't ranked. We compare your group with the World Bank's Global Jobs Indicators Database {{ cite("join") }}, which harmonises household and labour-force surveys into the share of employed people aged 15-64 in each group, also by sex and urban/rural. We use each economy's latest survey since {{ g.occupation.since }} and drop surveys that fail two plausibility checks: managers and professionals together exceeding both 10% of workers and 2.5 times the share of adults with post-secondary education (miscoded occupations), or farm and elementary workers falling under half of the ILO's agricultural employment share (a survey that misses rural areas). Dropped: {{ g.occupation.dropped | join(", ") }}. That leaves {{ g.occupation.economies }} economies with {{ (g.occupation.employed_covered / g.occupation.employed_world * 100) | pct(0) }} of the world's workers, pooled with employment weights (population 15+ times the ILO employment-to-population ratio). Because China is missing, the pooled shares are a reference group, not a world estimate. Your occupation's mid-rank by skill level, in the pooled group and in your country, is averaged with the status rank to give the work score.

6. Living standards

Seven household services. World, regional, national and urban/rural shares come from the World Bank's indicators, mostly for {{ inc.reference_year }} {{ cite("wdi") }}. The cited monitoring reports give the matching headcounts:

ServiceWorld Bank indicatorPeople withoutReport
{{ a.label }}{{ codes[key] }}{{ a.people_without | people }}{{ cite(a.cite) }}

If a share s of people lack a service, having it puts you at the mid-rank (s + 1)/2, and lacking it puts you at s/2. Your living-standards score is the average of these over the services you answered, using the world shares. This needs no assumption about how deprivations overlap. We also report a strict lower bound: the largest s among the services you have, since at least that many people lack something you have.

Limitations: "safely managed" water also requires water free from contamination, and sanitation requires safe treatment of waste, neither of which a respondent can verify. The health item simplifies SDG 3.8.2 and has no country breakdown.

7. Multidimensional poverty

The Global Multidimensional Poverty Index {{ cite("ophi_mpi") }} counts ten household deprivations. Each of three dimensions weighs a third, split equally among its indicators. We apply its cutoffs to your answers:

A score of a third or more counts as multidimensionally poor, a half or more as severely poor, and 20-33% as vulnerable. Indicators you skip are dropped and the rest of their dimension re-weighted; a skipped dimension is dropped and the other two re-weighted. At least four living-standards answers are needed. For context, the 2026 Global MPI counts {{ mpi.people | people }} people as multidimensionally poor in {{ mpi.countries }} countries {{ cite(mpi.cite) }}. The MPI is shown separately and isn't part of the SES index, because it overlaps the living-standards and education dimensions. We also flag overcrowding (more than three people per sleeping room) by the UN-Habitat standard {{ cite("un_habitat") }}.

8. Financial security

Gallup's Basic Needs Vulnerability study {{ cite("gallup_2020") }} asked adults in 142 countries whether they had been unable to afford food or shelter in the past 12 months, and whether they had friends or family to rely on. Gallup reports the high-vulnerability share ("about one in seven", 750 million adults); the 39% and 47% shares come from press coverage of the same index {{ cite("upi_gallup_2020") }}:

GroupDefinitionShare of adults

The Food Insecurity Experience Scale (8 yes/no items) {{ cite("fies") }} is scored with the common raw-score reading (0 secure, 1-3 mild, 4-6 moderate, 7-8 severe) {{ cite("fies_raw_score") }}. It's compared with FAO's estimates of severe and of moderate-or-severe food insecurity for the world and each country {{ cite("fao_wdi") }} (world, {{ g.aggregates.WLD.wdi["SN.ITK.MSFI.ZS"][0] }}: {{ g.aggregates.WLD.wdi["SN.ITK.MSFI.ZS"][1] | pct }} moderate or severe, {{ g.aggregates.WLD.wdi["SN.ITK.SVFI.ZS"][1] | pct }} severe; SOFI's latest headline is {{ (food.moderate_or_severe_share * 100) | pct }} for {{ food.year }} {{ cite(food.cite) }}). Your financial-security score is the average of your Gallup, FIES and Findex emergency-money mid-ranks (whichever you answered).

Financial resilience uses four Global Findex questions {{ cite("findex_2025") }}. Findex asks whether people could raise one-twentieth of their country's gross national income per person within 30 days and how difficult it would be; we show that amount in your currency (GNI per person from WDI, moved to today's prices, and only where its currency units match our price data). Your answer is ranked against the shares of adults giving each answer in your country and in the world ({{ g.findex.emergency.economies }} economies, {{ (g.findex.emergency.adults_covered / g.findex.emergency.adults_world * 100) | pct(0) }} of the world's adults; the world share is our average weighted by adult population), and this rank is the third part of your financial-security score. As context we also compare how long you could cover expenses after losing your main income (asked in {{ g.findex.cover_expenses.economies }} mostly low- and middle-income economies), the main source of emergency money, and your biggest financial worry. Findex reports the latest wave per economy (2024 for most, 2021 for many high-income economies).

We also show account ownership for the world and your country (Global Findex via WDI), including among people like you: your sex, your age group (15-24 or 25+), your education (primary or less, or secondary or more), and your household's income group (poorest 40% or richest 60%, from your national income rank). As context only, we also show saving. {{ (findex.saved_in_account_share_lmic * 100) | pct(0) }} of adults in low- and middle-income economies saved in a financial account {{ cite(findex.cite) }}.

Limitations: the Gallup data date from 2019, before the pandemic. The FIES raw-score bands only approximate FAO's probabilistic classification.

Social protection (context only)

You can tell us which public programmes your household received in the past year. The options follow the three programme types of the World Bank's ASPIRE database {{ cite("aspire") }}: social insurance (contributory pensions; unemployment, sickness, maternity and work-injury benefits), social assistance (cash transfers, family allowances, social pensions, food and other in-kind help, fee waivers) and labour market programmes (public works, training, job-search help). We compare your answer with the share of your country's population living in households that receive each type, from its latest household survey since {{ g.aspire_since }} ({{ coverage.social_protection }} economies), and with coverage in each fifth of the national distribution, highlighting the fifth your income falls in. ASPIRE ranks fifths by welfare before transfers, which is close to, but not the same as, the income you reported.

Globally, the ILO estimates that {{ (social_protection.covered_share * 100) | pct }} of people were covered by at least one social protection cash benefit in {{ social_protection.year }}, leaving {{ social_protection.uncovered_people | people }} with none {{ cite(social_protection.cite) }}. The ILO and ASPIRE numbers differ in concept: the ILO counts legal and effective coverage from administrative records, ASPIRE counts receipt reported in surveys. Receiving support reflects both need and access, so it isn't scored.

10. Your country (context only)

The results end with your country at a glance: its World Bank income group, GDP per person (and the share of the world living in poorer economies), the share of people below each poverty line, and key World Development Indicators, including inequality (Gini) and life expectancy for your sex, compared with your region and the world {{ cite("wdi") }}. It also shows the World Bank's Human Capital Index {{ cite("hci_2020") }} (the productivity a child born today can expect to reach given the country's health and schooling, relative to full health and complete education), learning poverty {{ cite("learning_poverty_2022") }} (the share of 10-year-olds who cannot read and understand a simple text), mobile subscriptions and health spending per person. None of this is part of your SES index: your income is already adjusted to your country's prices and compared globally, so counting the country again would double-count it.

11. The SES index

The SES index is the unweighted mean of the world percentiles you answered: income, education, work, living standards, financial security and, optionally, wealth. Averaging percentiles does not give a percentile, because no dataset measures all of these for the same people. Read the index as a summary of your position across dimensions. The income percentile is the best-measured single number.

12. Self-placement

The MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status {{ cite("macarthur") }} asks people to place themselves on a 10-rung ladder. It isn't scored. We show it next to your measured rung (your SES index divided into tenths) because people's sense of their standing is usually anchored on the people around them.

Languages

The quiz, results, questionnaire, FAQ and about pages are also available in Vietnamese. The Vietnamese survey wording is our own translation, not the official Vietnamese version of each instrument. Country names come from the Unicode CLDR (via Babel).

What changed from the original quiz

  • The old result averaged only the education and daily-income answers. Access and country were calculated but never used. All dimensions now count, and each is shown.
  • Missing commas in the old country lists silently merged "Lithuania" with "Switzerland" and "Thailand" with "Cuba". Trailing spaces and tabs ("India ", "Costa Rica ", "Jordan") and mismatched names (Burkina, Syria, El Salvador, Congo DR) left those countries unscored, and South Korea was listed as low income. Country data now come from ISO codes.
  • Daily income was asked in unadjusted dollars. It's now converted with each country's inflation and PPP factor and placed on the World Bank's global distribution, in the 2021 prices the World Bank adopted in June 2025.
  • The quiz no longer depends on a server-side session counter, which broke when users pressed Back or used two tabs.
  • Statistics were updated to the latest reports (2024-2026), and the questions use the surveys' own wording.

Reproducing the data

All country-level data are built by scripts/build_data.py directly from the World Bank (PIP and WDI) and IMF APIs, with the PIP release pinned, plus pinned commits of the Barro-Lee and ISO code files. A monthly GitHub Actions workflow reruns it with --latest (the newest PIP release, data year and IMF edition) and opens a pull request when the data change (see sources). The data were last built on {{ g.built }}. The cited global figures live in ses/facts.py. scripts/build_site.py renders this static site, and the scoring runs in your browser (static/js/ses.js), so your answers never leave your device.
